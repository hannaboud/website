RoyaltyChain

Every stream should pay everyone who made the song. Today that money can take two to three years to arrive, and some of it never does.

RoyaltyChain is a permissioned blockchain that becomes the music industry's single, shared record of who owns each song. When a streaming platform pays out, a smart contract splits the money and pays every rights holder at once, in USDC.

    Streaming rebuilt the music industry. The payment plumbing didn't change.

    Recorded music revenue fell for 15 years, then streaming brought it back to a record. In 2025, Spotify alone paid the music industry more than $11 billion. Getting that money to the right people is where the system breaks.

    One stream triggers three royalties

    The black box

    Royalties nobody can match to an owner pile up. In the US, streaming services handed $424 million of them to the Mechanical Licensing Collective (MLC). If the MLC can't match them within two years, it can hand the rest to publishers by market share, so the money goes to the biggest publishers instead of the people who wrote the songs. Independent songwriters, who can't register in every system, lose the most.

    One shared, tamper-proof record of who owns every song

    RoyaltyChain doesn't replace Spotify, Apple Music or any other platform. It replaces the ownership database underneath them, the layer where today's disputes and delays start.

    Why not a central database?

    The industry has tried that

    Shared registries like the Global Repertoire Database collapsed over one question: who controls the data. On a blockchain no single party owns the ledger, and no one can change a confirmed record alone.

    Why blockchain

    Nobody trusts anybody

    Labels don't trust publishers, publishers don't trust societies, and songwriters don't trust any of them. No single intermediary is accepted by everyone, so trust is replaced by cryptographic proof.

    Who runs it

    Every player is an equal node

    Labels, publishers, collecting societies and platforms each run a node with the same copy of the ledger. Smart contracts execute exactly as written and can't be lobbied or delayed.

    Why a permissioned chain, not Ethereum

    Royalties are a regulated financial flow full of confidential contracts, so a public chain is ruled out. RoyaltyChain runs on Hyperledger Fabric.

    Payments settle in USDC, a dollar stablecoin

    Splitting ownership is only useful if the money that arrives is real and stable. RoyaltyChain pays out in USDC, so songwriters anywhere can use it without exposure to crypto prices.

    1 USDC = $1

    Issued by Circle under US money transmission licenses, fully backed by dollar reserves and audited by independent accounting firms. The royalty is fixed in dollars at the moment it's paid, so there's no foreign exchange risk.

    Three layers, two API calls

    For a streaming platform, using RoyaltyChain looks like using any other API vendor. All the blockchain work happens underneath.

    LAYER 1Song registry

    When a song is created, every rights holder deploys one smart contract together. Once deployed it can't be changed unless all parties sign, and every transfer stays on the ledger.

    • Hash of the song's metadata, its unique fingerprint
    • Every rights holder's verified wallet
    • Every ownership percentage
    • The ISRC ↔ ISWC link
    LAYER 2Contract execution

    When a platform submits a royalty pool, the contract reads its ownership rules and pays every wallet in a single transaction.

    • Simultaneous payout to all holders
    • Timestamped and permanent
    • An audit trail anyone can verify
    LAYER 3API bridge

    Two endpoints in standard REST and GraphQL, speaking DDEX, so no platform has to reformat its data.

    # 1. on upload: look up the song's contract
GET  /songs/{isrc}  → contract address
# 2. each period: send the royalty pool
POST /contracts/{address}/pool

    A song's life on RoyaltyChain

    1. NewRegistration

      Before release, all rights holders register their wallets and agreed percentages. If they don't add up to exactly 100%, registration is rejected before any money is at stake.

    2. NewPlatform upload

      The song goes up with its ISRC as usual. The platform makes one extra API call and stores the contract address.

    3. UnchangedStreaming

      Listeners stream. The platform counts plays exactly as it does today.

    4. UnchangedRoyalty calculation

      At the end of each month, the platform computes the song's royalty pool with its existing model.

    5. NewSettlement

      The pool goes to the song's contract, which pays every wallet at once. Everyone is paid within minutes, with a full audit trail.

    The US exception

    Under the Music Modernization Act of 2018, platforms in the US must pay mechanical royalties to the MLC, not to rights holders directly. There, RoyaltyChain is the ownership registry the MLC queries to resolve splits instantly, instead of searching its own incomplete database. The money still flows through the MLC, by law.

    Mechanical royalties

    The main use case

    Outside the US, platforms send the mechanical royalty pool straight to the contract, which pays every wallet with no intermediary.

    Performance royalties

    PROs stay, the disputes go

    ASCAP, BMI, SACEM and GEMA keep collecting under their blanket licenses. Because every society reads ownership from the same record, the matching step that takes international payments 2–3 years disappears. In the US these royalties split 50/50 between writers and publishers, and independent writers who are their own publisher get both halves in one wallet.

    Value goes to creators instead of leaking along the chain

    Today value is taken at every intermediary, and the creator gets the rest late. With RoyaltyChain, money moves from the platform to the creator at settlement, and intermediaries keep their service fees but not the float. Every new song registered makes the shared database more useful to everyone on it.

    Who we onboard, and in what order

    Everyone from the biggest label to a session musician uses the same platform. Adoption has a chicken-and-egg problem, though: labels need platforms' revenue data, and platforms need labels on board to see the benefit. So we start with the platforms, beginning with a free pilot at one of the 20 major streaming services.

    1. Streaming platforms~$300Min legal settlements over historically unmatched royalties

      Spotify lists estimating royalties and identifying song ownership among its key risks, and the MLC has announced audits of Spotify, Apple Music and others for 2021–2023. Our pitch is regulatory relief: timestamped, immutable records.

    2. Major labels$10–30Myearly savings from a 1–3% efficiency gain

      Universal's publishing arm earned $1.37B from streaming in 2024 at a 22% EBITDA margin, so 78 cents of each dollar is cost. Labels can also promise artists consistent payment within minutes.

    3. Independents and societies36.09%independent market share in Q1 2024, above Universal's 29.35%

      Independent labels, self-released artists and collecting societies worldwide. Independents lose the most today: without a registered publisher entity, their publishing royalties vanish into the black box.

    4. Songwriters and crewFreeincluded through their organizations

      They don't pay, but they vote, can upload their own copy of a contract to check it against the label's, and get paid straight to their own wallets.

    A tiered subscription, not a fee per transaction

    The industry processes tens of billions of streams a year. Per-transaction pricing would swing with outside events and make it easy for clients to leave the moment a competitor appears. A tiered yearly subscription is stable for both sides.
