Price coherence in Kalshi prediction markets

Question. Some prediction market prices must agree with each other for logical reasons. A ladder of nested thresholds — "above 2.75%", "above 3.00%", "above 3.25%" — cannot price a higher bar above a lower one, since clearing a higher bar is never easier. How often does that fail, by how much, and how long does it last before someone fixes it?

Collectorrunning
Markets polled2,547
Interval15 s
Pairs tested697,532

Why the data has to be collected rather than downloaded

Kalshi's API reports the present, not the past. Historical trade prices exist, but historical bid and ask do not — and the executable test needs both. So the study is prospective: build an instrument, run it, report what it records. The dataset begins the day the collector starts.

Method

Poll top-of-book for every market in fourteen series every 15 seconds, storing only quotes that changed, with a full snapshot every hour as a checkpoint. Replay those changes to reconstruct the state of every market at every moment, then test each family's rungs pairwise in strike order.

Two tests. A soft violation is midpoints out of order — the markets are not watching each other, but the spread means nobody can profit. A hard violation means you could buy the underpriced rung at its ask, sell the overpriced one at its bid, and be guaranteed not to lose.

for i in range(len(present) - 1):
    (t_lo, k_lo, q_lo) = present[i]
    (t_hi, k_hi, q_hi) = present[i + 1]

    # A price with no size behind it is not a quote.
    bid_lo, bsz_lo = side(q_lo["yes_bid_dollars"], q_lo["yes_bid_size_fp"])
    ask_lo, asz_lo = side(q_lo["yes_ask_dollars"], q_lo["yes_ask_size_fp"])
    bid_hi, bsz_hi = side(q_hi["yes_bid_dollars"], q_hi["yes_bid_size_fp"])
    ask_hi, asz_hi = side(q_hi["yes_ask_dollars"], q_hi["yes_ask_size_fp"])

    # SOFT — midpoints must fall as the strike rises.
    if None not in (bid_lo, ask_lo, bid_hi, ask_hi):
        diff = (bid_hi + ask_hi) / 2 - (bid_lo + ask_lo) / 2
        if diff > 0:
            record("soft", magnitude=diff)

    # HARD — buy the lower rung at its ask, sell the upper at its bid.
    # Only those two sides matter; the other two quotes are irrelevant.
    if ask_lo is not None and bid_hi is not None:
        edge = bid_hi - ask_lo
        if edge > 0:
            record("hard", magnitude=edge, depth=min(asz_lo, bsz_hi))

Preliminary result

From roughly 83 minutes of collection on a Saturday afternoon. These rates are a first look at the shape, not a measurement — the sample is one slice of one day.

*Almost all of these are unusable. Of 1,667 hard violations, only six offered at least one whole contract; the rest sell into resting orders of 0.01 contracts. The median guaranteed profit is one hundredth of a cent, and the largest in the entire dataset is twenty cents.

Soft violations persist: median about one minute, a tenth of them past ten minutes, the longest 76 minutes. Only 1.6% disappear before the next poll, so these are not two order books updating out of step — they are sustained disagreements between markets that must agree.

Working conclusion: the ladder surface is frequently incoherent at midpoints and stays that way for minutes at a time, but the bid–ask spread absorbs essentially all of it. Executable incoherence is vanishingly rare. The market is not so much efficient as undisturbed — nobody enforces coherence because enforcing it does not pay.

Log

What broke, and how it surfaced. Every one of these was silent — the code ran without error and produced plausible numbers.

  • Sep 14 · classification Ladder families were being read as partitions, and vice versa.

    A temperature family is a partition of disjoint ranges, but its two end rungs are one-sided. A single open-ended rung flipped the whole family's label. The classifier collapsed rung types into a set, which answers "does any rung look like this" when the question was "do most of them". Fixed by counting the open ends directly: a partition has exactly two.

  • Sep 14 · universe selection The cleanest ladders were being excluded by the filter meant to find them.

    Kalshi marks mutually exclusive events with a flag, and I filtered on it. But nested thresholds overlap — score three goals and "1+", "2+" and "3+" all resolve yes — so the exchange correctly marks them not mutually exclusive. Filtering on the flag deleted 4,436 ladder families and kept 284 partitions.

  • Sep 14 · collection A third of cycles were being rate-limited invisibly.

    Cycle durations came in exactly two clusters, 0.85s and 2.85s, with nothing between. The two-second gap was the first step of my own backoff. Retries that eventually succeeded were logged nowhere, so the collector reported perfect health while being throttled. Fixed by spacing requests and recording retry counts.

  • Sep 15 · replay Restarting the collector made time appear to run backwards.

    Cycle numbers reset to 1 on restart, and cycle number is the ordering key for replay. Any violation spanning a restart would have been given a nonsense duration. Separately, a restart leaves no gap in the numbering, so the gap check reported clean coverage across an eleven-minute hole. Now gaps are detected on the clock, not the counter.

  • Sep 15 · detection 1,661 "guaranteed profit" opportunities offered zero contracts.

    The detector compared two prices without checking whether any order rested behind them. Kalshi reports a price field even when the side is empty. A guaranteed profit on zero contracts is not a thing that exists, which is what gave it away — the arithmetic was right and the economics were impossible.

  • Sep 15 · reporting The depth column printed 0 for orders of 0.01 contracts.

    Formatted with zero decimal places. I spent an hour diagnosing empty order books that were actually dust orders, because my own output rounded them away. Two separate bugs wore the same disguise.

Next

Move the collector off a laptop and run it unattended for three weeks. Thread wall-clock timestamps through replay so episode durations are measured in seconds rather than cycle counts. Exclude episodes that span a coverage gap. Then break persistence down by volume, rung spacing, and time to resolution.

Who actually owns US Treasury debt

Question. The published ownership tables stop at categories: "depository institutions", "mutual funds", "other investors". Those categories hide the thing worth knowing — which named institutions hold the debt, how concentrated that is, and how much of it sits with entities whose holdings are driven by regulation rather than preference. This goes underneath the aggregates using the filings those institutions already make.

US financial institutions — banks, funds, pensions, insurers — hold $9,071bn between them, close to the entire foreign holding. That bloc is the subject of the project.

Underneath the "depository institutions" line

FDIC call report data resolves that single $2,170bn row into named banks. Of 4,353 insured institutions, 2,231 hold any Treasuries at all, and together they account for $1,669bn — about 77% of the aggregate line.

The holdings are concentrated to a degree the aggregate row completely conceals. The Herfindahl index is 1,400, an effective count of about seven firms. Twenty-five banks reach 90% of all bank holdings. JPMorgan Chase alone holds $497bn and Bank of America $318bn — each larger than all but a handful of sovereign holders.

The gap matters as much as the number. Call Report Schedule RC-B excludes trading-account securities, so the roughly $500bn difference between the aggregate depository line and the sum of bank filings sits mostly in dealer trading books — meaning the dealer-affiliated banks, the ones that matter most for market functioning, are the ones most understated.

N-PORT is the important one. It carries par value, market value and maturity date per position, which makes it the only free source that yields a real holder-by-maturity grid rather than an assumed one. Money funds file N-MFP instead and hold roughly $2.5tn of bills, so leaving them out would gut any short-maturity analysis.

Log

  • Treasury Bulletin API Summing the latest data gave a total in the quadrillions.

    record_date is the Bulletin's issue date, not the period the data describes. Every quarterly issue republishes the full back-history, so filtering to the newest issue returns ten years of periods at once. The period lives in a separate column entirely.

  • Column mapping The holder column came back full of dates.

    The first version guessed at which column held the investor class and fell through to df.columns[1], which is the period. Replaced guessing with a schema inspection step that prints what the API actually returns before any parsing happens.

  • pandas 3.x Every column mapping silently matched nothing.

    The detection used dtype == object to find text columns. pandas 3 gives string columns a dedicated dtype, so that test matches nothing and the mapping fails without error. Now tested by exclusion — not numeric, not datetime, not boolean.

  • Period selection One quarter showed a single holder with a 98% share.

    OFS-2's investor-class detail derives from the Fed's Z.1 and lags the headline totals by a quarter, so the newest period carries only the fast-reporting classes. Taking the maximum period leaves two or three categories and a share that sums to nonsense. Now picks the newest period carrying a full set.

  • Aggregation Shares summed to well over 100%.

    The table nests: total public debt equals Fed and government accounts plus total privately held, and privately held is itself the sum of the detail rows. Mixing levels double-counts. Subtotals are now held back and used as a reconciliation check instead — if the detail does not tie to the published total, no shares get reported.

  • Scope Form 13F does not cover Treasuries.

    13F covers Section 13(f) securities — exchange-traded equities, options, convertible debt. Treasuries are not reportable. Worth writing down because it is the obvious first place to look for institution-level holdings and it cannot work.

Unresolved

"Other investors", $6,825bn. A residual holding individuals, GSEs, brokers, trusts and corporates. At 17.5% of all debt it is the third-largest line in the table and nobody knows what is in it. Z.1 is the route in.

"Mutual funds", $5,123bn. Blends money market funds holding bills under a year with bond funds holding the long end. Those are different investors with different behaviour, and the aggregate treats them as one. N-PORT and N-MFP separate them.

Bank trading books, roughly $500bn. The gap between the aggregate depository line and what the call reports show.

Next

Build the N-PORT loader and get a holder-by-maturity grid for funds, then the same for money funds via N-MFP. The question that grid answers is whether the maturity structure of the debt is matched by the maturity preferences of the people holding it, or whether it is being absorbed by whoever is obliged to hold it.
