A covered call holds 100 shares and sells a call against them. The Poor Man's Covered Call (PMCC) replaces the 100 shares with one deep in-the-money call that has about a year to expiry, and sells the same short call against it. The long call costs about a third of what the shares cost, which is the reason to use it.

This page backtests one algorithmic version on Eli Lilly (LLY) from to , using daily closing option quotes from LSEG, and compares it with buying and holding 100 shares. Both accounts start with the same cash.

Rules

Every trade follows from these rules. One option contract covers 100 shares. Two terms are used throughout: the entry day is the first trading day of a week (Monday, or Tuesday when Monday is a holiday), and the expiry day is the last trading day of the same week (Friday, or Thursday when Friday is a holiday), which is the day that week's listed option expires.

  1. Capital. On both accounts start with , the price of 100 LLY shares at that day's close. Buy and hold spends all of it on 100 shares. The PMCC spends on the long call and keeps the rest in cash, which earns a year.
  2. Long call. On the start date, buy one call expiring 18 Sep 2026, the listed expiry closest to 12 months away. The strike is the listed strike whose delta at the previous day's close is closest to , and never below . It is bought at the closing ask and held to expiry, where it is settled at its in-the-money amount (close minus strike). On the next trading day a replacement is bought by the same rule, expiring 17 Sep 2027.
  3. Trend signal. On the trading day before each entry day, compare LLY's close with the average of its last closes, that day included. Above the average is an uptrend; at or below is a downtrend.
  4. Target delta. in an uptrend, in a downtrend. A lower delta is a strike further above the stock price, which leaves more room for the stock to rise.
  5. Short call. On the entry day, take every listed call that expires on that week's expiry day and has a bid at the close. Sell one contract of the strike whose delta at that close is closest to the target delta. Delta is the value LSEG reports for the contract that day. The sale is filled at the closing bid.
  6. Expiry day. If LLY closes at or below the strike, the short call expires worthless. If LLY closes above the strike, the account buys 100 shares in the market at the close and delivers them at the strike, so it is never short stock. The cost is (close − strike) × 100.
  7. Nothing else. No rolling, no early closing, no stop-loss.
  8. Daily valuation. Cash, plus the long call at its closing bid, minus the open short call at its closing ask: the price to close each position.

Write-up

What the strategy is for

The PMCC is for someone who wants covered-call exposure to a stock without paying for 100 shares. Here the long call cost against for the shares. In exchange, the long call has an expiry date, loses time value, and pays no dividends.

What happened

The PMCC account returned and buy and hold returned . LLY rose over the year, and a strategy that sells calls gives up part of a rise like that.

How much capital it really takes

The long call cost , but the strategy could not have been run on that amount alone. When a short call finishes in the money the cost is paid in cash, while the offsetting gain stays inside the long call. An account funded with only the cost of the long call would have seen its cash fall to . On that basis the same trades return with volatility of and a drawdown of , but only by borrowing. That is why both accounts here start with the same cash: the PMCC account's cash never fell below , and all returns on this page use one capital base.

No look-ahead

The trend signal and the long call's strike use the previous trading day's close. The short call's strike is chosen from deltas at the entry day's close and sold at that same close's bid. This assumes the delta seen just before the close is the delta at the close. No data from after a trade is used to make it. Option contracts that had already expired were pulled individually from LSEG by contract code, so the backtest sees the strikes that were listed at the time.

Two rules were changed after the first run

The first version had a floor that kept the short strike above the long call's strike plus its price, and it chose the short strike from the previous day's delta. The floor left almost no premium in the weeks after a long call was bought, and the previous day's delta meant three calls were sold with the strike already below the stock price. Both rules were changed after seeing those results, which is a form of hindsight. For reference, the first version returned with a short-call net of .

Limitations

One stock, one year, and a year in which the stock rose strongly. The result says little about flat or falling markets, where selling calls usually does better.

Buy and hold excludes LLY's dividends, which were under 1% for the year. The PMCC receives none.

All fills are at daily closing quotes for one contract, with no commissions. The long call rarely trades, so its bid and ask are quotes, not trades.

Short calls are assumed to be assigned only at expiry. Early assignment is possible for American-style options and is ignored.

On an in-the-money expiry day the share purchase is larger than the account's cash. The purchase and the delivery happen at the same close, so only the difference is paid.

A few option quotes could not be retrieved from LSEG in May to July 2026 because of dropped requests. Every week still has at least 22 strikes to choose from.

Reproduce it

The code and the data it produced are in the repository. 04_pull_lly.py pulls the stock and option history from LSEG (it needs an LSEG Workspace login). 05_backtest.py runs the backtest from the saved CSV files and needs only pandas. 06_build_site_data.py writes the data file this page reads, so every number here comes from the backtest output.
