Project one

Price coherence in Kalshi prediction markets

Some prediction market prices must agree for logical reasons: a ladder of nested thresholds cannot price a higher bar above a lower one, since clearing a higher bar is never easier. The goal was to measure how often that fails, by how much, and how long it lasts before anyone corrects it.

One constraint shaped everything. Kalshi's API reports the present, not the past — historical trade prices exist, historical bid and ask do not. Since the executable test needs both, the data cannot be downloaded; it has to be collected going forward. That makes the collector the critical path, and every other script exists to keep it honest.

01

Find out what actually exists

Goal: get a complete picture of every open market, and learn the real shape of the data before writing anything that parses it.

The temptation is to read the documentation and write a parser against it. That fails quietly when the API has changed. This does the opposite: fetch everything, save it raw, then count which fields actually appear and how often. The field list it prints is what every later script gets written against.

It probes two hostnames rather than trusting one, because the first hostname I used did not resolve at all.

What this caught Prices do not arrive as yes_bid. They arrive as yes_bid_dollars, as strings, following an August 2026 format change. A parser written from the documentation would have returned nothing and raised nothing.

02

Work out which markets are logically constrained

Goal: separate ladders, where prices must be monotonic, from partitions, where prices must sum to a dollar.

Applying the wrong test makes every observation look like a violation, so this step decides what gets tested and how. It classifies by counting the shape of each family's rungs, and checks that family members share a settlement source and closing time — if they settle differently they are not comparable and the comparison means nothing.

Two mistakes here, both instructive The first classifier collapsed rung types into a set, which answers "does any rung look like this" when the question was "do most of them". A single open-ended rung flipped an entire family's label.

Worse: I filtered on Kalshi's mutually_exclusive flag to find ladders. But nested thresholds overlap — score three goals and "1+", "2+" and "3+" all resolve yes — so the exchange correctly marks them not mutually exclusive. The filter meant to find ladders was deleting 4,436 of them and keeping 284 partitions instead.

03

Build the instrument

Goal: record top-of-book for ~2,500 markets every 15 seconds, for weeks, unattended, without lying about what it saw.

Three decisions carry this. It polls the bulk endpoint rather than per-market order books, because price and size at top of book come back in one call and that is everything both tests need. It writes only quotes that changed, with a full snapshot every hour — 2,500 markets every 15 seconds is 9 GB over three weeks, and 96% of those rows would repeat the previous row exactly. And it writes a heartbeat every cycle recording what it saw, including failures.

The heartbeat is the most important part. Without it, an outage during a live violation records as one long violation — a fabricated data point in the headline metric, produced by a monitoring system reporting perfect health.

The one I nearly missed Cycle durations came in exactly two clusters, 0.85s and 2.85s, with nothing between. Network jitter is continuous; this was not. The two-second gap was the first step of my own retry backoff — a third of cycles were being rate-limited, retrying successfully, and logging nothing.

04

Rebuild the full picture from the changes

Goal: turn a stream of edits back into "what did every market look like at this moment", and prove the reconstruction is correct rather than assume it.

Storing only changes is a large saving and a large risk: a bug here silently corrupts everything downstream. Which is why the collector writes an hourly full snapshot. This script carries state forward from the deltas and compares it against that snapshot — if a quote ever changed without a row being written, the two disagree and it says so.

It also reports gaps in coverage, which is subtler than it looks. A restart leaves no hole in the cycle numbering, so a counter-based check reports clean coverage across an eleven-minute hole in the clock. Gaps are detected on timestamps, not counters.

Verified, not assumed At the first scheduled checkpoint, 2,541 markets reconstructed from deltas matched the snapshot exactly. Zero mismatches.

05

Test the constraint

Goal: for every family at every moment, check the prices step down as they must — and separate "the prices disagree" from "you could have made money".

A soft violation is midpoints out of order: the markets are not watching each other, but the spread means nobody can profit. A hard violation means you could buy the underpriced rung at its ask, sell the overpriced one at its bid, and be guaranteed not to lose.

The hard test needs only those two sides, not all four quotes — you do not need to know what someone would pay for the rung you are buying. That keeps thin markets in the sample instead of excluding exactly the population where violations are most likely.

No magnitude threshold is applied. Every violation records its size and depth, so a filter can be chosen later with the distribution in view. A threshold baked into detection cannot be undone.

Where economics beat testing The detector reported 1,667 guaranteed-profit opportunities. It compared two prices correctly and had no notion that a price means nothing without an order behind it. A guaranteed profit on zero contracts is not a thing that exists in the world — which is what gave it away. No test of the comparison logic would have caught it, because the comparison was never wrong.

06

Interrogate the result before believing it

Goal: find out whether the violations describe something that could actually happen.

A rate on its own is not a finding. This prints the distribution of magnitude, depth, dollar value and episode length, plus how concentrated the violations are across events — a result that lives in one market is a quirk rather than a property.

And a bug in the inspector itself Depth printed as 0 for orders of 0.01 contracts, because I formatted it with zero decimal places. I spent an hour diagnosing empty order books that were really dust orders. Two separate bugs wearing the same disguise — and the second was in the tool built to catch the first.

07

Confirm the results came from the current code

Goal: rule out the possibility of analysing a stale output file.

After a fix, one set of numbers changed and another did not, which could not be true of a single run. Rather than reason further, this checks file timestamps, then imports the detector from disk and feeds it a known case to see what it actually does. The results file turned out to predate the fix by three hours.

Worth keeping permanently. Comparing runs is meaningless if you cannot establish which code produced them.

Project two

Who actually owns US Treasury debt

The published tables stop at categories — "depository institutions", "mutual funds", "other investors". Those categories hide the thing worth knowing: which named institutions hold the debt, and how concentrated that is. The approach takes the aggregate table as a frame, then goes underneath each line using the filings those institutions already make.

01

Get ownership by investor class

Goal: a clean quarterly series of who holds Treasury debt, from the Treasury Bulletin.

This leads with an --inspect mode for the same reason the prediction-markets recon does: the first version guessed which column held the investor class and was wrong. Inspecting the schema before parsing turns a silent wrong answer into a visible one.

Three traps in one table record_date is the Bulletin's issue date, not the period the data describes, and each issue republishes the full back-history — so filtering to the newest issue returns ten years of periods at once and summing them gives a total in the quadrillions.

The investor-class detail derives from the Fed's Z.1 and lags the headline totals by a quarter, so the newest period carries only two or three classes and a share that sums to nonsense. The script picks the newest complete period instead.

The table nests: total public debt equals Fed and government accounts plus total privately held, and privately held is itself the sum of the detail rows. Mixing levels double-counts, so subtotals are held back and used as a reconciliation check — if the detail does not tie to the published total, no shares are reported at all.

02

Go underneath the aggregate lines

Goal: resolve "depository institutions, $2,170bn" into named banks, and set up the same for funds and dealers.

Each holder category has a different regulatory filing underneath it. Banks file call reports, collected by the FDIC. Registered funds file N-PORT, which carries par value, market value and maturity date per position — the only free source that yields a real holder-by-maturity grid rather than an assumed one. Money market funds are excluded from N-PORT and file N-MFP instead, and since they hold roughly $2.5tn of bills, omitting them would gut any short-maturity analysis.

The docstring also records what does not work, which saved real time: Form 13F covers exchange-traded equities and options. Treasuries are not reportable on it. It is the obvious first place to look and it cannot work.

What the aggregate was hiding Of 4,353 insured institutions, 2,231 hold any Treasuries, together $1,669bn. The Herfindahl index is 1,400 — an effective count of about seven firms. JPMorgan Chase alone holds $497bn.

The missing $500bn matters too: Call Report Schedule RC-B excludes trading-account securities, so the understatement falls specifically on dealer-affiliated banks — the ones that matter most for market functioning.

03

Assemble one picture

Goal: put the aggregate and the named institutions side by side, and state plainly what is still unresolved.

This fetches nothing — it reads what the other scripts produced. If a file is missing, that section is skipped and flagged rather than silently dropped, so the output never looks complete when it is not.

It ends with the section that matters most. "Other investors" is $6,825bn, the third-largest line in the table, and it is a residual containing individuals, GSEs, brokers, trusts and corporates. "Mutual funds" at $5,123bn blends money funds holding bills with bond funds holding the long end — different investors, different behaviour, one row.

04

Draw it

Goal: two figures that carry the argument — where the debt sits, and how concentrated the bank slice is.

Both default to PDF because vector text stays sharp in LaTeX. Units convert on read: the FDIC reports in thousands, so everything becomes billions immediately and the axis labels are honest. The ownership chart highlights the US financial institution bloc; the bank chart flags dealer-affiliated institutions, since those are the ones the data understates.
