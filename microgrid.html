How much solar should a town of 2,000 build?

We sized a solar panel and storage microgrid in MATLAB, then tested it against 300 scenarios of uncertain loan rates, grid prices and carbon fees.

Step 1

Sizing the problem

Hot Springs, North Carolina runs a real solar microgrid. We used it as the template for a town of 2,000 people, and first needed two inputs: how much energy the town uses, and how much land there is for panels.

Demand

Each resident draws between 500 and 1,000 W on average. We took the midpoint and scaled it to a full day for the whole town.

\[\bar P=\tfrac12\,(500+1000)=750\ \text{W/person}\] \[\begin{aligned}E_{\text{day}}&=0.75\ \text{kW}\times 24\ \text{h}\times 2000\\&=36{,}000\ \text{kWh}\end{aligned}\]

The brief asked for one full day of demand in reserve, so 36,000 kWh is also the storage target.

Site area

We traced the outline of the existing Hot Springs array on satellite imagery. The dark rows inside it are the shadows the panels cast, not panels, so we measured each shadow row and subtracted it.

\[\begin{aligned}A_{\text{site}}&=36{,}873\ \text{m}^2-\textstyle\sum\text{shadow rows}\\&=14{,}800\ \text{m}^2\end{aligned}\]

The shadows covered more than half of the outline. Keep the 14,800 m² in mind: several designs later want three to four times more land.

Step 2

The optimization model

The model has two design variables and three uncertain prices. For any choice of panel area and storage size, it simulates every hour of a year and returns the average cost of each kWh the town consumes.

\(A\)
solar panel area, m² (design variable)
\(C\)
storage capacity, kWh (design variable)
\(r_\gamma\)
construction loan interest rate, 4% to 9%
\(r_u\)
utility price of grid electricity, $0.09 to $0.15 per kWh
\(r_o\)
carbon offset fee on each kWh of grid power, $0.10 to $0.90 per kWh
\[\begin{aligned}\min_{A,\,C}\quad& J(A,C;\,r_\gamma,r_u,r_o)\\ \text{subject to}\quad& 150\le A\le 60{,}000\ \text{m}^2\\ & 280\le C\le 112{,}000\ \text{kWh}\\ & g_1\le 0,\quad g_2\le 0\end{aligned}\] \[J=\frac{L+M+(r_u+r_o)\,E_{\text{grid}}}{E_{\text{demand}}}\]

Here \(L\) is the yearly loan payment on the panels and storage (it grows with \(A\), \(C\) and \(r_\gamma\)), \(M\) the operating cost and \(E_{\text{grid}}\) the energy bought from the grid (it shrinks as \(A\) and \(C\) grow), so \(J\) is the cost of each kWh in cents. Every kWh bought from the grid pays both the utility price and the carbon fee, so those two prices push in the same direction. Building more panels and storage replaces those fees with loan payments, and the optimizer looks for the point where the two balance. We solved it with sequential quadratic programming (SQP), starting from 15,000 m² and 28,000 kWh.

Reading the Lagrange multipliers

In the first runs both variables ended on their lower bounds with positive multipliers, \(\lambda_A\approx 20.0\) and \(\lambda_C\approx 3.8\). A positive multiplier on a lower bound means the cost was still falling there: the optimizer would have built even less if the bound allowed it. The two inequality constraints had \(\lambda=0\) in every run. They never bound, so each design was set by the cost tradeoff and the variable bounds alone.

How energy is routed each hour

Inside the simulation, a fixed dispatch rule decides where each hour's energy comes from. Solar serves the town first, any surplus charges storage, storage covers a shortfall, and the grid supplies whatever is still missing.

\[P_{sd}=\min(P_s,\,P_d)\] \[P_{sx}=\min\!\Big(P_s-P_d,\;p_{\text{in}}C,\;\tfrac{s_{\max}C-E}{\eta}\Big)\quad\text{if }P_s>P_d\] \[P_{xd}=\min\!\Big(P_d-P_s,\;p_{\text{out}}C,\;\eta(E-s_{\min}C)\Big)\quad\text{if }P_d>P_s\] \[P_{ud}=P_d-P_{sd}-P_{xd}\]
\(P_{sd},\,P_{sx}\)
solar to demand, solar into storage
\(P_{xd},\,P_{ud}\)
storage to demand, grid (utility) to demand
\(E,\;\eta\)
energy currently stored, round-trip efficiency of the storage technology
\(p_{\text{in}},\,p_{\text{out}}\)
maximum charge and discharge rate per kWh of capacity
\(s_{\min},\,s_{\max}\)
lowest and highest allowed state of charge
Step 2 · Technology

The technology grid

Three panel types times four storage technologies gives 12 designs. We optimized each one at baseline rates: grid power at 12¢ per kWh, a carbon fee of 40¢ per kWh and a 6.5% loan. Choose a measure to shade the grid, and select a design for its details.

Li-ion = lithium-ion battery · PSH = pumped-storage hydro · CAES = compressed-air energy storage. “Over site” marks designs that need more than the 14,800 m² we measured.

What the grid shows

The cheapest designs don't fit. At these rates the lowest costs come from low-efficiency panels with pumped hydro (34.2¢) or compressed air (33.6¢). Both fill the 60,000 m² upper bound, about four times the site.

High-efficiency panels aren't built at all. Area and storage stay at their floors, and the cost is just the grid price plus the carbon fee: 12¢ + 40¢ ≈ 52¢. At baseline rates the expensive panels never pay for themselves.

Bulk storage wins every row. Pumped hydro and compressed air were the two cheapest storage options for every panel type.

Step 3

Monte Carlo under uncertain prices

A single run answers one set of prices. We ran the optimizer under random prices in two stages: first to see which technology wins, then to measure how uncertain the cost of the chosen design is.

Stage 1 · Which technology wins (100 scenarios)

For each scenario we drew the three rates uniformly across their ranges, optimized all 12 panel and storage pairs, and kept the cheapest. That is 1,200 optimizations in total.

\(r_\gamma\sim\mathcal U(0.04,\,0.09)\)\(r_u\sim\mathcal U(0.09,\,0.15)\)\(r_o\sim\mathcal U(0.10,\,0.90)\)

The winning designs fell into three groups, set by the grid price and the carbon fee:

Both prices low

grid under ~13¢ and carbon fee under 55¢
  • About 150 m² of high-efficiency panels and 0.3 MWh of compressed-air storage
  • Solar covers only 8 to 10% of demand
  • Lowest cost of the three groups

One price high

grid over ~14¢ or carbon fee over 55¢
  • 55,000 to 60,000 m² of panels and 15 to 18 MWh of compressed-air storage
  • The grid supplies under 3% of demand
  • Higher cost, driven by the capital spend

Both prices high

grid near 15¢ and carbon fee past ~60¢
  • Panels at the 60,000 m² cap and more than 20 MWh of storage, usually pumped hydro
  • The grid supplies under 1% of demand
  • Highest cost: the town is nearly off-grid

Stage 2 · How uncertain is the cost (200 scenarios)

With the design fixed to high-efficiency panels and compressed-air storage, we drew each rate from a lognormal distribution, since real rates are positive and skewed to the right. Each one is centered on the middle of its range, with a spread that puts the ends of the range about two standard deviations out. The three rates were drawn independently.

\(\tilde r=\tfrac12\,(r_{\max}+r_{\min})\)\(c_r=\dfrac{r_{\max}-r_{\min}}{4\,\tilde r}\)\(R=I_3\)

The carbon fee dominates. As it rises, the optimizer pays more up front to avoid it, so the cost climbs steeply until the system is built out. The loan rate matters moderately, since it sets the cost of every panel and kWh of storage. The grid price barely registers, for two reasons. Its typical spread was only about ±1.5¢ around 12¢, against roughly ±20¢ around 50¢ for the carbon fee, and both apply to the same grid kWh. And whenever the carbon fee is high, the optimizer avoids the grid, which removes the grid price from the cost too.

How the design responds

Panel area behaves like a yes-or-no decision. With the narrower Monte Carlo bounds, nearly every run ends at about 1,000 m² or about 30,000 m². A high carbon fee with a moderate loan rate means full investment; a low fee with an expensive loan means the floor and grid power.

Storage is the tuning knob. It often sits at its floor too, but when it is built it mostly lands between 10,000 and 20,000 kWh, sized to how much daytime solar has to be shifted into the night.

The final design

High-efficiency solar panels with compressed-air storage

Land The site has 14,800 m². High-efficiency panels produce the most energy per square meter, while the cheaper low- and standard-efficiency builds needed 43,000 to 60,000 m² in the technology grid.
Storage Compressed air was the cheapest or nearly cheapest storage for every panel type, and the usual pick in the Monte Carlo scenarios that built out solar.
Risk It is not the cheapest choice when carbon is cheap. It is built for the scenarios where carbon fees rise, which is where the cost risk is.

Next step. Our model gives surplus solar no value. If the town could sell excess energy back to the grid, even below the retail price, oversizing would stop being a penalty. That would push more designs toward a full build and make the cost less sensitive to the carbon fee and the loan rate.

The MATLAB code behind the results. SolarMicro_opt runs the hourly simulation and the SQP optimization for one set of rates.

Control logic: dispatch inside the hourly simulation MATLAB 
% use solar to meet demand when available; prioritize charging storage with any excess
if (Pd < Ps)
Psd = Pd;
Psx = min([Ps - Pd, max_P_I_X * cap_E_X, (maxCharge * cap_E_X - E) / Efficiency_X]);
% if solar falls short, use storage to cover the remaining demand
if (Pd > Ps)
    Psd = Ps;
Pxd = min([Pd - Ps, max_P_O_X * cap_E_X, (E - minCharge * cap_E_X) * Efficiency_X]);

%if solar + storage still cant meet demand, draw remaining energy from the grid
if Psd + Pxd < Pd
Pud = Pd - Psd - Pxd;

Monte Carlo, stage 1: 100 scenarios MATLAB 
clear; clc;

N = 100;   % Number of iterations

solarTypes   = 1:3;     % 1=low, 2=standard, 3=high-eff
storageTypes = 1:4;     % 1=Li-Ion, 2=Flywheel, 3=PSH, 4=CAES

results = []; %starts with initialize the list

for i = 1:N
    % Random financial parameters in their valid ranges, choosing uniformly
    utilRate = 0.09 + rand() * (0.15 - 0.09);   % Uniform between 0.09 and 0.15
    ghgRate = 0.10 + rand() * (0.90 - 0.10);    % Uniform between 0.10 and 0.90
    loanRate = 0.04 + rand() * (0.09 - 0.04);   % Uniform between 0.04 and 0.09
    rates = [utilRate; ghgRate; loanRate];

        best_cost = inf;
        best_result = [];

        for st = solarTypes
            for xt = storageTypes
                try
                    [f_opt, ~, v_opt] = SolarMicro_opt(rates, 0, xt, st);

                    global avgPud_per_h % import global grid use
                    if f_opt < best_cost
                        best_cost = f_opt;
                        best_result = [utilRate, ghgRate, loanRate, st, xt, f_opt, v_opt(:)', avgPud_per_h];
                    end
                catch
                    fprintf('Error for combo xt=%d, st=%d\n', xt, st);
                    continue;
                end
            end
        end

    results(end+1,:) = best_result;
end

% Display the results
colnames = {'utilRate','ghgRate','loanRate','solarType','storageType','cost_c/kWh','Area_m2','Storage_kWh','GridUse_kWh/h'};
disp(array2table(results, 'VariableNames', colnames));
