Part 1How the wheel works

Two states, two trades

The account is always in one of two states, and each state has exactly one trade. Every week, at the close of its first trading day (usually Monday), it sells one SPY option expiring at the close of the next week's first trading day, so each option is held for one week, weekend included.

State A · cash, no shares
Sell 1 cash-secured put at the ~-delta strike
↻ Finishes out of the money: keep the premium, sell another put next week
Put finishes in the money: assigned, buy 100 shares at the strike
Call finishes in the money: called away, sell 100 shares at the strike
State B · 100 shares
Sell 1 covered call at the ~-delta strike, never below the shares' cost
↻ Finishes out of the money: keep the premium and the shares, sell another call

Leg 1: the cash-secured put

Selling a put means taking on an obligation: if SPY closes below the strike K at expiry, the account must buy 100 shares at K. In exchange the buyer pays a premium P up front, which the seller keeps whatever happens. "Cash-secured" means the account holds 100 × K in cash the whole time, so the purchase can always be paid for: no borrowing, no margin call.

P&L at expiry, per contract = 100 × ( P − max(K − ST, 0) ) breakeven = K − P best case = 100 × P worst case = 100 × (K − P), if SPY went to 0

The seller wins if SPY stays above the breakeven, and loses one-for-one below it, so the premium only cushions a fall. In practice nothing is "lost" at assignment; the account simply owns shares bought at K while SPY trades lower, and moves to State B.

Leg 2: the covered call

With 100 shares, the account sells a call: an obligation to sell the shares at K if SPY closes above it at expiry, in exchange for a premium C. "Covered" because the shares to deliver are already owned. Measured from the week's opening price S0:

P&L at expiry, per contract = 100 × ( (ST − S0) + C − max(ST − K, 0) ) upside capped at 100 × (K − S0 + C) downside = the shares' fall, cushioned by C

The call strike is never set below what the shares cost (put strike − put premium), so being called away never locks in a loss on the shares. The price of that rule is visible after a selloff: the nearest call at or above cost can sit far above SPY and pay almost nothing.

Why it is really one trade: put-call parity

For the same strike and expiry, a call and a put are tied together by C − P = S·e−qT − K·e−rT. Rearranged, owning the shares and selling the call has the same payoff as holding cash and selling the put:

S − C = K·e−rT − P + (S − S·e−qT) covered call cash-secured put dividend over the week (cents)

Tick the overlay box above and the two lines sit on top of each other. So the wheel never really switches strategies: it is short one put the whole year. What changes at assignment is which put. In State A it is an out-of-the-money put (about shares of SPY exposure on average). In State B a -delta call on 100 shares is the same as a short in-the-money put (about shares). The wheel's market exposure more than doubles right after SPY falls through a put strike.

The Greeks of a short weekly option

The Greeks measure how the position's value responds to each thing that moves it, here from the seller's side, per contract. The curves use Black-Scholes at a typical setting from this backtest: SPY at , implied vol , strike ( delta with 7 days left). Each line is a different number of days to expiry.

Delta (shares of SPY the position behaves like). Positive: the seller of a put is long the market. Far above the strike it is near 0; far below, near 100. Close to expiry the change from 0 to 100 becomes a cliff right at the strike.

Gamma (how much delta changes per $1 in SPY). Always negative for a seller: the position gets longer as SPY falls and shorter as it rises, the wrong way both times. It spikes at the strike as expiry nears: weekly options are mostly a gamma bet.

Theta (dollars earned per day as time passes, all else equal). Positive for the seller, largest at the strike, and accelerating into expiry. This is what the seller is paid.

Vega (dollars per 1-point rise in implied vol). Negative: the seller loses when volatility rises. It shrinks as expiry nears, so a weekly option is far less exposed to vol than to the spot moves that gamma measures.

The trade in one sentence: the wheel collects theta and pays for it in gamma. It makes money when SPY moves less than the option price implied. Part 3 measures exactly that split on the real positions.

Part 2Thesis, then the result

The thesis

Why it should make money. The wheel works like an insurance company. Every week I sell one option on SPY and collect the premium up front: a put while I hold cash, a call while I hold shares. At 0.25 delta, each option starts with approximately a 25% chance of finishing in the money, so most weeks it expires worthless and I keep the whole premium. But winning most weeks is not what makes it profitable, because when an option does finish in the money, the payout is usually bigger than the premium. What actually makes money is when the volatility implied by option prices is higher than the volatility SPY actually delivers. Theta is the premium I earn every day, gamma is the claims I pay when SPY moves, and the wheel works when the first is bigger than the second.

What it should beat. I expect a better Sharpe ratio than buying and holding SPY, but not necessarily a higher return. The wheel holds less than 100 shares' worth of exposure most of the time, which should lower volatility, while the premiums add income. The upside is capped: in a strong rally I only keep the premium and the gain up to the strike, so it should lag SPY in a big up year. It can beat SPY's return when the market goes sideways or drifts down, especially if it moves less than options priced in. Big swings hurt it, because they trigger payouts and whipsaw: assigned after a drop, then called away on the bounce. That is what I saw with IBM in my covered-call regime analysis.

What would prove it wrong. Two results would contradict this thesis:

  1. The options pay out more than they collect over the year, so the option leg loses money. That would mean implied volatility was not above realized.
  2. The wheel's Sharpe ratio comes in at or below SPY's.

A weaker test: in a year when SPY rises strongly, the wheel should not beat it on return. If it does, the cap cost less than expected, and I need to explain why. Either way, one year can be consistent with this thesis but cannot prove it: a Sharpe ratio estimated from one year is uncertain by roughly ±1.

What happened

Same start for every column. SPY 100 sh holds the same maximum share count as the wheel, with the rest in cash. SPY all-in holds as many whole shares as the capital buys. Both earn the same interest on cash as the wheel ( a year) and reinvest their dividends in SPY on the pay date. Sharpe = (annualized return − risk-free) / annualized volatility; risk-free = the same cash rate.

Market exposure: position delta

Shares of SPY the account behaves like. Put phase in blue, call phase in pink; SPY in the strip above. The jumps are assignments: exposure rises right after SPY falls through a put strike and drops after a call-away, which is the short-put-the-whole-time view from Part 1.

P&L attribution: theta earned vs gamma paid

From one close to the next, four things change the price of the option the account is short. The chart splits each day's gain or loss between them:

  • Time (theta). With one day less to expiry, the option is worth a little less. The seller earns that every day.
  • Direction (delta). SPY going up or down. A short put gains when SPY rises; a covered call gains on the shares.
  • Size of the move (gamma). Big moves cost the seller more than the direction alone suggests, whichever way SPY goes. These are the insurance claims.
  • Implied volatility (vega). When the market prices in less volatility, the option gets cheaper and the seller gains; when it prices in more, the seller loses.

The four are measured one after the other with Black-Scholes, so together they add up to almost exactly what the option's price actually did. The commission paid at each sale is shown separately.

Fill assumption, and the data behind it

Assumption: every option is sold at the closing mid, (bid + ask) / 2, of the day it is sold, minus a commission per contract, which is the market convention for options at US retail brokers. Open options are valued at the mid. Shares change hands only through assignment, at the strike.

Why it is safe for SPY. Across daily quotes on near-the-money weeklies (|delta| 0.15 to 0.45), the median spread is , and on the options the wheel actually sold it is , about of the premium. Selling at the bid instead of mid would have cost over the whole year, against of premium at mid.

Why mid and not the bid. The day's last trade (TRDPRC_1) printed at the middle of the spread on median (position , where 0 = bid and 1 = ask), and mid explains trade prices with R² = (slope ), so options really do trade around the mid.

Caveat: daily bars pair the day's last trade with the closing quote, which are not simultaneous, so some prints fall outside the closing bid and ask ( below the bid, above the ask). Prints repeated from the previous day (no trade that day) were dropped.

Write-up

Why SPY

The wheel only makes sense on something you are happy to own after a put is assigned, and an index fund removes single-company risk. SPY options are also the most heavily traded in the US market, so the fill assumption is easy to defend (see Fills).

How the strike is chosen

The strike is the listed strike whose delta, at the close the option is sold, is closest to . Delta is roughly the market's probability that the option finishes in the money, so a fixed delta takes the same amount of risk every week whether volatility is high or low; a fixed percentage out of the money would not. 0.25 keeps the risk moderate: closer to the money pays more but gets assigned more; further out pays too little to matter. On the one entry close where LSEG had no deltas (Oct 12, 2026, Columbus Day), the delta is computed with Black-Scholes from that close's mid.

The cost-basis floor on calls

After assignment, calls are never written below what the shares cost (put strike minus put premium), so a call-away never locks in a loss on the shares. The cost is visible in the weekly table: after a drop the nearest call at or above cost basis is far out of the money and pays very little, so the wheel can sit on shares for weeks collecting almost nothing until SPY recovers.

No rolls, no early closes

Every option runs to expiry. Rolling or buying back early is a second, discretionary decision layered on the wheel; leaving it out keeps the test about the wheel itself and keeps every blotter row tied to a rule.

Early assignment and dividends

SPY options are American-style and SPY pays a quarterly dividend. On the day before an ex-dividend date, a call holder who is in the money gains by exercising early when the dividend is worth more than the call's remaining time value. The backtest leaves this out: options are assigned only at expiry. This year it would not have mattered, because on the eve of every ex-dividend date either no call was open or the open call was out of the money. Cash, interest and Reg T

Every put is fully cash-secured: the account holds the whole strike in cash, which is why the starting capital is (SPY's highest close in the year was near $779, so one put needed up to about $78,000). Cash earns a flat a year, accrued daily and credited monthly, because a broker pays interest on cash collateral and leaving it out would understate a strategy that is mostly cash. Reg T is used rather than portfolio margin because it is a fixed, published formula; no position here uses leverage.

How the Greeks are computed

Black-Scholes-Merton with a continuous dividend yield. Implied vol is solved from each closing mid (bisection), with time measured in calendar days to the 4:00 pm expiry over 365. The P&L attribution reprices the option one step at a time (time, then spot, then vol), so its pieces add up to the actual change in the quote. On the closing option quote sat below the lowest price Black-Scholes allows (S·e−qT − K·e−rT for a call) against SPY's close (SPY options keep trading 15 minutes after the shares close), so no volatility can reprice it; that is nearly all of the residual.

Limitations

Marks are daily closes; nothing between closes is seen. Assignment is decided on the closing price; after-hours moves that change a holder's exercise decision are ignored. Black-Scholes assumes constant volatility and no jumps, so the Greeks are local, model-based measures, not exact. One year is a short sample: the standard error of a Sharpe ratio estimated from one year is roughly 1, so the comparison is evidence, not proof. Taxes and assignment fees are ignored, and cash earns a flat rate all year.
